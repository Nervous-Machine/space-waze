One superstorm. Three satellites. Both kinds of truth.

In May 2024 the Gannon G5 superstorm — the strongest in two decades — hit everything in orbit at once. ESA’s three Swarm satellites flew straight through it. From their data we earned two different certainties: what the storm did to the sky, cell by cell — and whether each sensor could be trusted while it happened. Where those two questions meet is where the most dangerous edge cases live.

Where the dangerous edge cases live.

A reading that violates expectation has three explanations: the sky changed, the model is wrong, or the sensor is lying. On a quiet day you can tell them apart. Inside a superstorm, the lie hides in the noise — unless you know, cell by cell, what the storm should be doing.

Same satellites. Same storm. Same kernel. The voxel map and the trust verdicts are two readings of one learned causal structure — the intersection isn’t a hand-off between systems, it’s where one system does its best work.

How the cross works.

Step 1 · on the spacecraft

An earned link is violated

A high-certainty causal link sees a residual it can’t explain. The flag fires in-cycle, on-device — no ground loop, no retraining.

Step 2 · against the map

The atlas is consulted

What should this storm be doing in this cell? If the certified response — or a standard-model failure flag — explains the residual, it’s attributed and margins are sized.

Step 3 · across the fleet

The fleet votes

Peers in neighboring cells see the same sky. If the sky explains it, everyone saw it. If only one node saw it — the sensor is lying.

The verdict — storm, model failure, or compromised sensor — is attributed in real time, auditable, with no phone-home.
