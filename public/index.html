A map of certainty in space.

Space operations run on models that predict density, dose, charging. Good ones. But a model can’t tell you what it doesn’t know. That has to be measured from the outside. From LEO to the Mars surface, this atlas draws the frontier by calculating certainty where it can be earned from measurement, and recording gaps where it can’t. With five decades of model heritage, LEO is the most mature environment, but even there, storms locally drive the standard model's error to ten times its everyday miss. We flag these errors about three days ahead, at 92% precision, where drag residual is available from live satellites. The gaps are even bigger in GEO, cislunar, and the Mars corridor.

Deep dive: how the LEO density grid was earned ↗
Every dashed cell is a satellite-shaped hole in the map. The signal that fills it isn’t telemetry or proprietary state — it’s the drag residual a satellite’s GPS already computes for navigation. Three source bands lit their columns; a contributing fleet fills the grid wherever it flies. We also audit the baseline model itself: its solar-EUV term is corrected in 13 of 13 cells out-of-sample, and its failure windows are flagged in real time at 92% precision (the audits ↗).

MEASURED — faster solar wind reliably pumps up the electron flux about two days later, in all four local-time sectors, strongest on the midnight side — exactly the shape spacecraft-charging planning needs, and we called the ordering before we saw it. We trust the direction and the ordering; we do not yet claim the amplitude. A longer observing window earns that.

MODELED, UNVERIFIED — one longitude sees each sector only ~6 storm-hours a day, so the storm side cannot be certified from measurement yet. MEASURED — we followed the Moon's radiation dose across 17 years and learned how it answers solar-particle floods; the storm-vs-quiet model structure we built for it held over the entire archive. Two honest caveats we keep on the books: extreme floods are so rare that no one can yet be confident about them, and one geometry effect (the magnetotail's influence on dose) behaves opposite to what the textbooks suggested — an open question we publish rather than hide.

Mars orbit

Mars surface
Mars surface
MEASURED — surface dose followed across two separate years (2023 and 2024–25). The dependable part: solar-particle events SUPPRESS dose at the surface — the atmosphere filters the flood — the same way in both years, and in one season we certified the effect is essentially zero, firmly (knowing a solid zero is real knowledge). We also caught two solar-storm arrivals at Mars that appear in no official catalog, confirmed against an orbiting spacecraft. The puzzle we publish alongside: a seasonal dose signal shows up in BOTH years and has survived every explanation we tested — our best open mystery, with the tests on record.

Operational outlook · 2026–2027 and beyond

What this map means for the missions ahead.

An honest map earns its keep when you plan against it. Here is how the atlas reads against what’s actually on the manifest — which numbers you can schedule around, which are stakes awaiting a score, and which are still holes.

2026–2027 · Constellations · Stations · Reentry Certified to 550 km

LEO: the next few thousand satellites deploy into the dashed cells.

The big deployments of the next two years — Kuiper’s shells at 590–630 km, Starlink’s continuing build-out, and everything that rides with them — fly almost entirely in the 550–850 km rows of the grid above: the rows where nobody is measuring and the standard density models disagree with themselves by 4–13%. Storm-time drag error lands directly on conjunction screening and reentry prediction, and the declining phase of Solar Cycle 25 is historically rich in the recurrent high-speed streams that drive it.

What the atlas already offers that fleet: below 550 km — where the first commercial stations and most crewed LEO operations fly — storm response is certified cell by cell, and when the standard model is about to fail, we flag it in real time at 92% precision. The rest is closable: a fleet that shares the drag residual its GPS already computes lights up its own corridor.

2026–2027 · GEO fleet · Declining phase Storm side staked

GEO: a two-day heads-up on charging, earned on the calm side.

The declining phase of the solar cycle is when high-speed streams recur every solar rotation and GEO’s relativistic-electron episodes — the spacecraft-charging environment — are historically at their strongest. The earned part: faster solar wind pumps electron flux about two days later, in all four local-time sectors, strongest on the midnight side — an ordering we called before we saw it. That is a usable two-day heads-up for anomaly attribution and scheduling charging-sensitive operations.

The unearned part, said plainly: the storm-time response is modeled, not certified — one longitude sees each sector only ~6 storm-hours a day — so storm charging margins should stay conservative until additional GEO longitude feeds close the cell.

2026–2027 · Artemis · Gateway · CLPS landers Surface archive blocked

The Moon: dose behavior is earned; the surface archive is the cheapest close.

As crews return to cislunar space — the Artemis flights, Gateway assembly, a steady lander cadence through 2027 — the atlas holds 17 years of measured lunar-orbit dose response to solar-particle floods, with the storm-vs-quiet structure intact across the whole archive. Two caveats matter operationally. Extreme floods are so rare that nobody can certify shelter margins for them from data — margin there is philosophy, not measurement. And the magnetotail’s effect on dose runs opposite to textbook expectation; the Moon spends days of every month inside it, so for a long-stay base that is a real open question about the monthly dose rhythm — published rather than hidden.

For the surface itself: the instrument exists (LND), the archive is blocked, and one data agreement converts base-siting dose assumptions from inference to measurement. It should happen before anyone pours regolith.

Late-2026 window · Earth→Mars transit · Surface Corridor unmeasured

The corridor: crews will spend months in the one region never continuously measured.

Every crewed Mars architecture spends six to nine months each way in the transfer corridor — and four one-way snapshots in 25 years is the entire measurement record. Our stake, on the books: ~0.28–0.29 mGy/day of free-space galactic-cosmic-ray dose (silicon), which compounds to roughly 50–80 mGy per leg before a single solar storm — if the stake is right. That “if” is the point. The late-2026 Mars departure window is the nearest chance to close the biggest hole on this map: any rideshare hosting a small dosimeter, reporting hourly for one solar rotation, scores the stake. Analysis pre-committed on our side.

At Mars, the measured results already carry weight for a base: ~20 g/cm² of atmosphere erases the electron-driven dose signal seen in orbit — a flight-data anchor for habitat shielding mass — and solar-particle events suppress surface dose rather than spike it, the kind of fact a storm-shelter strategy can be sited on. One honest mystery stays on the books: a seasonal surface-dose signal that has survived every explanation we’ve tested.

The bigger picture

Assured autonomy starts with navigating an evolving ecosystem.

Nervous Machine learns the causal drivers of local environments — whether they live inside a machine like a satellite, a drone, a robot, or a quantum computer, or in an open environment like space. We believe the intersection where internal and external drivers influence each other is where some of the most unexpected edge cases arise — and that assurance there has to be earned, not assumed.

This atlas is that conviction applied to the largest open environment there is: certainty earned from measurement, models stamped and staked where they stand in, and every hole named alongside the measurement that closes it.
