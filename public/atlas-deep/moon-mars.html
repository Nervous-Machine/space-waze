Cislunar · Moon · Transit · Mars

The question: beyond the magnetosphere, radiation is the constraint. What do we actually know about dose at the Moon, on the way to Mars, and at Mars — and what is still guesswork?

The Moon (17 years of measurements)

Earth's alerts are not enough at the Moon: we found solar-particle events that produced order-of-magnitude dose responses at the Moon while Earth's official catalogs stayed silent — missed by threshold (too small at Earth), by latency (arrived at the Moon first), and by curation (never entered the record). Each was confirmed by an independent spacecraft in lunar orbit. If you operate near the Moon, you need lunar-vicinity monitoring, not just Earth-side alerts.
This solar cycle is different: counting the same way across 17 years, the quiet-day radiation bursts that seeded those discoveries are overwhelmingly a current-cycle phenomenon — 44 of 60 burst-days live in 2023–2025, and 2012 (a loud year by Earth standards) has zero. Models tuned on past cycles will miss this Sun.
The storm/quiet split holds: one structural change — letting storm hours and quiet hours carry separate weights — fixed a 18× storm-hour error and stayed fixed across the entire archive. The magnetotail's effect on dose still behaves opposite to textbook expectation; that stays an open question, published, not hidden.

Mars (two years, measured on the ground)

The atmosphere filters the flood: solar-particle events suppress surface dose at Mars — the same direction in both measured years — and in one season we certified the effect as essentially zero, firmly. (A confident zero is real knowledge: it bounds the hazard.) The same solar events push dose up at the Moon: same Sun, opposite signs, ordered by shielding. One rule, planetary bodies as its coordinates.
Two uncataloged storm arrivals at Mars: our residuals flagged them, and upstream plasma data from an orbiting spacecraft confirmed both, to the day — arrivals no official catalog lists.
The best open mystery: a seasonal dose signal at Gale Crater appears in both measured years and has survived every explanation we tested — it is not calibration, not daily storm bookkeeping, not maskable. It waits on a dust-opacity data release or a Mars upstream monitor; our prediction for the ESCAPADE mission (~2028) is already staked in advance.
